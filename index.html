Three numbers worth knowing

The bar is lower than the nerves suggest.

Most of the fear around an entry test comes from not knowing where the line sits. Here it is, and here is the deliberately harder line this app holds you to instead.

40%
What the university asks
Sixteen questions out of forty. That is the real condition on the offer letter, and nothing more.
60%
What this app asks
Twenty points of margin, on purpose. Nerves, a misread question and a rushed final ten minutes have to fit somewhere.
80%
What unlocks the test
The timed paper stays locked until the training set comes back at this. Not strictness — sequence.

Sitting a timed paper before the methods are automatic teaches you nothing except how it feels to run out of time. That is the whole reason the second step is shut.

How it works

Two steps, and the second one stays shut.

01

Train, as many times as you like

Each question is marked the instant you answer it, with the full working shown before you move on. No clock. The order of the questions changes every run, and so does the order of the four answers, so you practise the method instead of remembering where the right one sat.

—

Score 80% and the door opens

The timed test is locked until the training set comes back at eighty per cent. Each subject unlocks on its own: clearing maths does not open English.

02

Sit the test properly

Forty different questions — none of them from the training set — with the clock running down and the paper submitting itself at zero. Your camera can sit in the corner and time away from the screen is counted, so the conditions are honest. Nothing is recorded. Afterwards you get every answer, every explanation, and a breakdown of which topics cost you marks.

03

Every attempt is kept

Nothing is thrown away. Each attempt is numbered, timed and stored with its topic breakdown, so the record shows a trend rather than a mood. It also ranks the areas that keep costing you marks across every attempt you have ever made — which is usually a shorter list than it feels like.

Worth doing

Four things worth more than another hour of revision.

01

Keep paper beside you

The test is sat online, but the working still belongs on paper. Read on the screen, calculate on the page — and practise that way from the start, not for the first time on the day.

02

Slow down

An hour and a half for forty questions is over two minutes each. Time is not the constraint. Carelessness is. There is room to work every answer twice.

03

Read the wrong answers

Each wrong option is a real mistake, and the explanation says which one. Understanding why an answer is tempting beats three more attempts.

04

Ask the admissions office

One question is still open: is a calculator allowed, and is it your own or one built into the screen? Worth a short email now rather than a surprise on the day.

The first run will be uncomfortable. That is the point.

Nobody clears sixty questions first time. The score matters far less than what you do with the ones you got wrong.
